# Which short course is worth your time? Start with the job you want

You've found a course. The description sounds promising, the certificate looks professional and there's a discount that ends soon.

You're tempted. Doing something feels better than waiting for an opportunity, especially when your job search has been quiet or your career feels stuck.

Before you enrol, ask one question: what do I want to be able to do when I finish?

That question can save you money and weekends. A course may be useful, but "add another certificate to my CV" doesn't tell you whether it's the right one for the work you want.

## Choose a role to investigate first

You don't need a complete five-year plan. Pick one realistic role or a responsibility you'd like to take on in your current job.

Read several recent adverts for that work. Write down the skills and tasks that keep appearing. Look at what employers want someone to do, rather than collecting every tool or qualification mentioned once.

For an office support role, you might notice repeated requests for spreadsheet work. Look more closely. Are they asking for accurate data entry, formulas, reporting or something more advanced? "Learn Excel" becomes a more useful goal when you know what you need to do with it.

If you're still choosing a direction, the [career-change guide](/insights/career-change-south-africa-before-you-leave) can help you research the work before you pay to train for it.

## Read the course outline past the headline

Find out what you'll actually practise. Does the course include exercises? Will anyone give feedback? Are there assessments, or do you receive a certificate for watching the material?

Check the starting level too. A course described as "beginner-friendly" may still assume knowledge you don't have. Ask the provider what you should already know and whether there is a sample lesson.

Think about how you learn. If you're comfortable working independently, recorded lessons may suit you. If you often get stuck without someone to ask, support and feedback may matter more than having a large library of videos.

Also check the equipment and software requirements. A course that is technically available on your phone may still involve practical work that's difficult to complete without a computer.

## Understand what the certificate means

A certificate of completion can show that you finished a course. A vendor certification may involve a separate assessment set by a software or technology provider. A registered qualification or part-qualification has its own formal status. These are different things, even when the marketing makes them sound similar.

If a provider claims a programme leads to a registered qualification, ask for its exact name and SAQA ID, then check the [SAQA qualification register](https://regqs.saqa.org.za/). SAQA is the South African Qualifications Authority. Check the provider's authorisation to offer that particular qualification with the quality council responsible for it as well: the QCTO for occupational qualifications, the CHE for higher education qualifications or Umalusi for school and further education qualifications.

Don't assume every course offered by an accredited institution has the same status. SAQA's [guidance on quality qualifications](https://www.saqa.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Quality-Qualifications.pdf) explains why the particular qualification and provider need checking.

A short course can still teach a useful practical skill without being a registered qualification. The question is whether it meets your purpose. If a job requires a particular professional qualification, a general short course won't automatically meet that requirement.

## Add up the full cost

Look beyond the advertised fee. Is the assessment included? Will you pay separately for the certificate, software, textbooks or a retake? How long will you have access to the material?

Data and transport count too. So does the time you can realistically give it. A course that needs ten hours each week will be difficult if you have two hours available after work and family responsibilities.

Read the cancellation and refund terms before paying. If the provider promises employment, ask exactly what that means and request the conditions in writing. Help with applications and a guaranteed job are very different commitments.

You can use the [free learning resource guide](/insights/free-sa-learning-resources-2026) as a starting list, but check the provider's current terms. Free access, certificates and funding offers can change.

## Decide what you'll be able to show

Imagine an interviewer asking, "What did you learn, and where have you used it?" Plan for that answer before you start the course.

For a spreadsheet course, you could build a simple stock tracker with fictional data. Check that entries are consistent, create a summary and explain how someone would use it. For a writing course, you might produce a short set of instructions and ask someone to test whether they can follow them.

Label practice work honestly. Don't describe a fictional project as paid client work. Keep employer and customer information out of it unless you have the appropriate permission.

A finished piece of work gives you something concrete to discuss. You can explain where you struggled, what you corrected and what you'd still like to learn. That is more informative than listing a course title and hoping the employer fills in the rest.

## Compare two options on one page

Before deciding, write down the following for each course:

- The task I want to learn to do.
- The lessons and exercises that teach that task.
- The feedback or support available when I get stuck.
- The certificate or qualification awarded, and what I've verified about it.
- The total cost and the hours I can give it each week.
- The piece of work I expect to finish.

You may find that a free introductory course is enough for your next step. You may find that paying for feedback or a recognised assessment makes sense. Choose on the basis of the gap you're trying to close, rather than the size of the discount.

## What if you've already collected several certificates?

You don't need to dismiss the learning you've done. Go back to one course and ask how you can use it.

Could you complete a practice project, apply the skill in an agreed task at work or explain it clearly enough to help someone else? You might need more practice before you need another course.

If you're unsure which skill would help your next move, a [Career Clarity Session](/book/clarity) can help you connect your experience, target roles and learning choices. For today, find one advert for work you'd like to do. Circle one task you need to learn, and use that as the starting point for your course search.
