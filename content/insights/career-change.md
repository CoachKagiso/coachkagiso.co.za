# Changing careers at 30, 35 or 40: how to test a move before you leave

You can be grateful for your job and still wonder how long you can keep doing it.

Maybe you've stopped learning. Maybe the work has never felt right, but there was always a reason to stay. Now you're looking at another career and thinking about the years you've already invested in this one.

Then come the practical questions. Would you need another qualification? Could you afford a lower salary? Would anyone take your experience seriously in a different field?

Those questions deserve more than "It's never too late". You need enough information to decide what kind of change makes sense for your life. You can begin collecting it while you're still employed.

## Work out what you actually want to change

Before choosing a new career, separate the work from the circumstances around it.

Would you enjoy the same role with a different manager? Is the commute taking more out of you than the work itself? Are you interested in a different kind of work, or do you mainly need more responsibility and better pay?

Write down what you want less of and what you want more of. Be specific. "I want to be happy" is understandable, but it won't help you compare two roles. "I want more time solving problems and less time making repetitive calls" gives you something to investigate.

You may discover that a move within your organisation would help. You may realise the whole field is wrong for you. Either answer is useful. The [career-planning guide](/insights/career-planning-when-you-feel-stuck-south-africa) can help if you're still trying to name what's bothering you.

## Take your experience apart before you dismiss it

It's easy to reduce ten years of work to a job title. Look at the tasks underneath it.

Perhaps you've handled difficult customers, trained new starters, kept records accurate or coordinated people who had different priorities. Those skills may be useful elsewhere, although a new role could still require specialist knowledge or a formal qualification.

Here's an illustrative example. Someone in customer support wants to explore training coordination. They have helped colleagues learn a new system and written instructions that the team uses. Those experiences give them a starting point. They still need to find out whether target roles involve organising training, designing learning material, facilitating sessions or all of those things.

Write down a skill, the example that proves it and a role where it might be useful. Keep your original job title honest. You can explain relevant work without relabelling yourself as someone you've never been.

## What changes at 30, 35 or 40?

Your age doesn't decide whether a change is possible. It does change what the move costs and what you bring to it.

At 30, you may still have room to study part-time, take a junior role or try something for a year and change your mind. You also have fewer years of evidence to point to, so a practical project can carry more weight.

By 35 or 40, there's often more relying on your income: a bond, school fees, family members you support. You also bring more experience of handling people, pressure and responsibility. That makes adjacent roles worth a serious look. A move that uses half of what you already know may get you there with less of a pay drop than starting from the bottom.

An employer may wonder whether someone with your experience will settle into a more junior role. Answer that directly in your application. Explain why you're choosing the move and how you'll handle being the newer person in the room.

## Check what the new work is like on an ordinary Tuesday

A job can look appealing from a distance. Before committing to it, find out what people actually spend their time doing.

Read several recent adverts for the same kind of role. Note the repeated tasks, entry requirements and tools. Separate requirements that appear consistently from an unusual wish list in one advert.

Speak to someone doing the work if you can. Ask what takes up most of their week, what newcomers underestimate and how people usually enter the field. "What do you find frustrating about the work?" may tell you more than asking whether they enjoy it.

You don't have to ask for a job. A short, respectful request for information is enough: "I'm exploring a move from customer support into training coordination. Would you be open to answering two questions about the day-to-day work? I understand if you're unable to."

One person's experience won't describe every workplace. Compare what they tell you with adverts and other conversations.

## Try a small version of the work

Look for a way to test the activity before paying for a long course or leaving your role. You might shadow someone with permission, take on a relevant internal project or complete a small practice task using fictional information.

For the training example, that could mean preparing a short guide and asking someone to use it. Did you enjoy explaining the process? Could they follow it? What needed changing?

Respect your current responsibilities and any restrictions on outside work. Don't use confidential employer or customer information in a practice project or portfolio. A fictional example can still demonstrate your thinking.

Keep the experiment small enough to finish. You are trying to learn whether the work suits you and what you need to develop. A half-finished collection of courses may tell you less than one completed piece of work and useful feedback.

## Put the practical constraints on paper

If other people rely on your income, that matters. So do transport, study time, care responsibilities and the cost of any required qualification.

Find out what entry into the new role could involve. Can you make an internal move? Are there adjacent roles that use more of your existing experience? Would you be starting at a lower level, and is that manageable for your household?

Write down the costs you can identify and the questions you still need answered. Avoid building the plan around a salary you've seen once online or an assumption that work will follow immediately after a course.

If the move depends on a professional registration or qualification, confirm the requirements with the relevant official body before enrolling. A short course can be useful, but it won't substitute for every formal entry route.

## Give yourself a month to learn something concrete

During the first week, choose one possible direction and collect a small set of relevant adverts. In the second, speak to people who understand the work. Use the third week for a practical task or an introductory learning activity. In the fourth, review what you've found.

Ask yourself: Do I still want the work now that I understand it better? Which parts of my experience transfer? What is the smallest sensible next step? What would make me pause?

You might decide to continue, explore an adjacent role or leave this option alone. None of those outcomes means the month was wasted. You have information you didn't have before.

## Explain the move as a considered choice

When you start applying, connect your existing experience to the new role. "I need a change" explains your feeling. The employer also needs to understand what you can contribute.

You could say: "My customer support work included helping new colleagues learn our systems. That led me to explore training coordination. I've since completed a small learning project and spoken with people in the field, and I'd like to build on that experience in this role."

Adapt that example to what you've actually done. You don't need to apologise for having a career before this one.

If you want help comparing your options, a [Career Clarity Session](/book/clarity) can give the decision some structure. For now, choose one role to investigate. You can take the question seriously without making your whole life change this week.
