# How to network for a job when you don't know anyone in your industry

"You need to network."

People say it as though there is a room somewhere where useful contacts are waiting for you to arrive. Meanwhile, you're trying to work out who to message and how to avoid sounding as though you're asking a stranger to solve your life.

If networking feels awkward, start smaller. You can ask a thoughtful question, reconnect with someone you already know or join a conversation about work that interests you. You don't need a large circle before you can begin.

The first aim is to understand the work and build a relationship that both people are comfortable with. A job lead may come from that. It may not. The conversation can still help you make a better decision.

## Be clear enough for someone to help

"I'm looking for anything" is an honest description of how desperate a job search can feel. It's also difficult for another person to act on.

Give them a starting point: the kind of work you're exploring, the experience you bring and the question you have.

For example: "I'm looking into office support roles. I've handled scheduling and customer queries, and I'd like to understand what employers expect from junior administrators."

That doesn't lock you into one career forever. It makes this particular conversation easier. If you're exploring several directions, have a clear question for each rather than asking one person to advise on everything.

Your [LinkedIn profile](/insights/linkedin-profile-optimisation-south-africa-2026) can back up the message. When someone looks you up, they should be able to understand the experience you're referring to.

## Begin closer to home than you think

Make a short list of people you've studied with, worked with, volunteered alongside or met through a professional event. You may not have spoken for a while. That's okay; acknowledge it naturally.

You can also look for alumni groups, professional associations and public events relevant to your interests. Check whether there is a free or online option before committing money or travel time. You don't need to buy access to an expensive conference to have a useful conversation.

Choose people because there is a sensible connection to your question. A person doing the work you want to understand may be more useful than the most senior person you can find.

## Send a message that is easy to answer

The examples below are fictional messages you can adapt. Replace the details with your own and write in a way you'd actually speak.

To a former colleague: "Hi Lerato, it's been a while. I hope you're well. I'm exploring coordinator roles and remembered that you moved into operations. Would you be comfortable telling me which skills helped you make that move? I'm trying to understand what I should work on next."

To someone from your college or university: "Hi Thabo, I noticed we both studied at the same institution. I've recently completed my qualification and I'm exploring office support roles. If you have a moment, I'd appreciate your view on one thing: what do new starters often underestimate about the work?"

To someone whose public work you've followed: "Hi Ayesha, your post about training new staff caught my attention. I'm in customer support and have been helping colleagues learn our system. I'm considering training coordination. Would you be open to one or two questions about the day-to-day work? No problem if you're unable to."

Each message explains the connection and makes a small request. There is no need for a long introduction, flattery or an unexpected CV attachment.

## If they say yes, respect the time they've offered

Prepare a few questions you couldn't easily answer by reading their profile. Ask what the work involves, how people usually enter the field or which skill would be useful to practise first.

If you agree on a fifteen-minute call, keep an eye on the time. You can say, "We're nearly at the time we agreed. May I ask one last question?" Let them decide whether to continue.

Listen closely enough to ask a follow-up about what they've said. You don't have to work through a script of questions if the conversation has already answered them.

Avoid asking for confidential details about their employer, applicants or colleagues. You can learn a lot about a field without putting someone in an uncomfortable position.

## When is it okay to ask about opportunities?

If there is a specific vacancy and the conversation makes it appropriate, you can ask about the application process: "I noticed the coordinator vacancy on your company's careers page. Do you know whether applications should go directly through that page?"

A referral is a bigger request. The person may not know your work well enough to recommend you, or their employer may have a particular process. Give them an easy way to say no.

"If you feel you know my work well enough, would you be comfortable referring me? I understand if you'd prefer that I apply directly."

Apply through the required route either way. A conversation doesn't replace the employer's process, and knowing someone doesn't guarantee an interview.

## Follow up without chasing

If an initial message gets no reply, one brief follow-up after a week or two is a reasonable personal approach. You could say, "Just following up on my message in case it got buried. I understand if now isn't a good time."

Then leave it there. People may be busy, away or unable to help. Repeated messages can turn a small request into pressure.

After a useful conversation, send a specific thank-you. "Your explanation of the reporting work helped me understand what I need to practise" means more than a copied paragraph of praise.

If you later use their advice, a short update can keep the relationship going naturally. You can also share something useful when it fits, but you don't need to manufacture a favour to repay them immediately.

## Make room for this in your actual week

Try a small routine you can sustain: choose one person, read enough to make a relevant approach and send a thoughtful message. Keep a note of any reply and the next step you agreed on.

Continue applying for suitable roles. Networking is another route to information and relationships, not a reason to stop the rest of your search. If applications are going quiet, the [job-search check](/insights/applying-for-jobs-no-interviews-south-africa) can help you review that part too.
