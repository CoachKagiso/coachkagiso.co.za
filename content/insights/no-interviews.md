# Why am I applying for jobs but getting no interviews?

You've changed your CV. You've uploaded it again. You've filled in an application form that asks for everything already on your CV. Then you check your inbox, and there's nothing.

After a while, the silence starts to feel personal. You begin wondering whether your experience counts for anything.

Before you rewrite the whole document again, let's look at where your search might be getting stuck. Your CV matters, but it is one part of a process you don't fully control. The role you're choosing, the evidence you give and the way you apply all deserve a closer look.

You can do this check with one recent application. Open the advert beside the CV you sent. That's where we'll start.

## Are you applying for work you can explain your fit for?

When you need a job, being told to narrow your search can sound ridiculous. Of course you're open to different things. You have bills.

You can keep more than one option open. What helps is giving each option a clear reason to consider you.

For each advert, write down the main things the person will need to do. Look beyond the title. Two jobs called "administrator" can involve very different work: one might centre on diaries and correspondence, another on stock records and supplier follow-ups.

Next to each requirement, write a real example of when you've done something similar. If the advert asks for a specific licence or qualification, check whether it is essential. A willingness to learn won't replace every entry requirement.

You don't have to match every preference. You do need to explain why the work is within reach. If that explanation is difficult, pause before spending another hour on the application. You may need a different role, a clearer example or some experience you haven't built yet.

## Does your CV show the work, or only name it?

"Responsible for administration" might be accurate. It also leaves someone guessing what you actually handled.

Here's an illustrative rewrite: "Maintained stock records, followed up missing delivery documents and prepared the weekly order summary for the supervisor."

That gives a reader something to picture. It doesn't need a dramatic percentage or an award to be useful. Only use details you can stand behind.

Read the first half-page of your CV. Would a stranger understand the work you want and the experience that supports it? Or would they need to piece your story together from five different jobs?

Put the most relevant evidence where it's easy to find. Keep your real job titles, but explain the responsibilities that connect to the vacancy. Use the employer's terms when they accurately describe your experience. Don't add a skill simply because it appears in the advert.

If you're worried about the file itself, the [ATS CV guide](/insights/ats-cv-south-africa-4-minute-fix) covers formatting checks. An ATS is the software some employers use to manage applications. Passing a formatting check doesn't guarantee an interview, but it can help you catch avoidable problems.

## Check the boring things before changing everything

Open the exact file you submitted. Is it the right version? Does it open properly? Are your phone number and email address correct? Did the application ask for a document you forgot to attach?

Check your voicemail and spam folder too. If someone calls during work hours, have a short response ready: "Thanks for calling. I'm unable to speak privately right now. Could we arrange a time later today?"

These details won't solve a difficult job market. They are worth checking because they're fixable, and they're easy to miss when you're tired.

## Where are you looking?

If every application comes through one app, try adding a second route that suits your field. That could be an employer's own careers page, a relevant recruitment agency or a conversation with a former colleague.

When a vacancy appears on a job board, check the employer's official application instructions. Use the stated route. Sending the same application to every address you can find may create confusion rather than help.

You can also make it easier for people to understand what you're looking for. "Please let me know if you hear of anything" gives them very little to work with. "I'm looking for office support roles where I can use my scheduling and supplier coordination experience" is easier to remember.

Your [LinkedIn profile](/insights/linkedin-profile-optimisation-south-africa-2026) can support that message. It should tell the same basic story as your application.

## Keep a record that helps you make decisions

You don't need a complicated spreadsheet. A notebook is enough. For each application, record the role, employer, date, CV version and how far you got. Add a short note about why the role suited you.

After a few weeks, look for patterns. Are you getting calls for one type of role but silence for another? Are you reaching interviews, then going no further? Those are different problems and need different work.

Use this small worksheet for your next application:

- The main work in this role is...
- My strongest matching example is...
- The requirement I'm least clear about is...
- The change I'll make before applying is...
- The response or next step was...

There isn't a universal number of applications you should send before an interview arrives. Comparing your search with a stranger's total can make a hard week worse without telling you what to change.

## What if you've done all of this?

Then it may be time for another pair of eyes. Ask someone to compare one advert with your application and tell you where your fit becomes unclear. A specific question will usually help more than "Is my CV okay?"

There will also be things you can't see: another candidate's experience, an internal decision, a changed budget or a paused vacancy. Silence doesn't tell you which of those happened. It certainly doesn't tell you your worth.

Choose one useful change for your next few applications and keep a record of what happens. That gives you something to learn from, even when the process feels frustratingly quiet.
