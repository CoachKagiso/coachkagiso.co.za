# How to prepare for a job interview using the experience you already have

The interview invitation arrives and, for a moment, you're relieved. Someone wants to speak to you.

Then you start preparing. You find a list of common questions, open another tab, watch a video and suddenly have forty possible answers to remember. By the time someone asks you to describe yourself, you've forgotten how you normally speak.

Let's make this more manageable. Start with the job and a few real examples from your experience. You'll have something solid to talk about even when the question comes in a form you didn't expect.

You don't need to become a different person for the interview. You do need to make your experience understandable to someone who wasn't there.

## Read the advert as a description of someone's working day

Go back to the job description. What will this person be expected to handle? Perhaps they'll deal with customer complaints, organise deliveries, check records or coordinate a small team.

Choose the responsibilities that seem central to the role. For each one, ask yourself: "When have I done this, or something close to it?"

An employer asking for "attention to detail" might need someone who catches mistakes before an order goes out. Saying you're detail-oriented doesn't give them much to assess. Describing how you spotted a mismatch, checked it and corrected the order does.

Make a simple note with two columns: "What the role needs" and "An example I can discuss". If one requirement has no example beside it, think about what you do know and how you'd explain the gap honestly. Don't create experience to fill the space.

## Choose stories you remember clearly

Think of a time you solved a problem, worked through a disagreement, learned something unfamiliar or corrected a mistake. Choose examples where you can explain your own part.

If you're early in your career, your examples may come from a group assignment, volunteering, part-time work or helping in a family business. Say where the experience came from. It can demonstrate a useful skill without being presented as a full-time job.

You might have heard of STAR. It stands for situation, task, action and result. In ordinary language: what was happening, what needed doing, what you did and how it turned out.

Use that structure to keep yourself on track. You don't need to announce each letter or make every answer sound like it came out of a textbook.

## Give your actions enough space

Here's an illustrative example for a customer support interview:

"A customer contacted us because a delivery hadn't arrived when expected. I checked the order record and found that the address had been captured incorrectly. I confirmed the details with the customer, contacted dispatch and agreed on a new delivery time. I then called the customer back with the update. The order was delivered, and I flagged the capture error to my supervisor so we could check how it had happened."

That answer gives the interviewer something to ask about. How did you handle the customer's frustration? What did you do while waiting for dispatch? What would you change next time?

Notice that the example doesn't end with a made-up satisfaction score. A result can be an issue resolved, a deadline met or a mistake caught. If you have reliable numbers, use them. If you don't, explain the outcome you actually know.

When reviewing your own answers, check how much time you spend describing the background. A short explanation is usually enough. Leave room for the decisions you made and the actions you took.

## Practise speaking, not only writing

An answer that looks clear on a page can become a very long story out loud. Record yourself answering one question on your phone. Listen once, without picking apart every pause.

Could someone follow what happened? Did you explain your contribution? Did you answer the question? Choose one thing to improve and try again.

Keep brief notes rather than memorising a script. You want to remember the example well enough to adapt it. If the interviewer asks about teamwork, you'll explain how you worked with others. If they ask about a mistake, you may use the same experience but focus on what went wrong and how you responded.

If you lose your place in the interview, you can pause: "Let me give you a clearer example." You can also ask them to repeat or clarify a question. A short pause gives you a chance to answer the question they actually asked.

## Prepare an opening and a few questions of your own

For "Tell me about yourself", practise a brief introduction that connects your current experience to the role. Give them a useful starting point, then let the conversation develop. You don't need your entire life story in the first answer.

Read the employer's official website and the information they've sent you. Look for what the organisation does, who it serves and where this job fits. You don't need to pretend you've admired the company since childhood.

Prepare questions you genuinely want answered. "What would you want the person in this role to have achieved after three months?" can help you understand expectations. "What is the biggest challenge the team is dealing with?" may tell you something the advert doesn't.

If salary comes up, be clear about the basis of any figure you discuss and ask what the package includes. It's reasonable to need details before you can compare an opportunity properly.

## Sort out the practical details early

Confirm the time, location and format. For an in-person interview, check the route and allow for transport delays. Keep the contact person's details somewhere you can find them quickly.

For a video interview, test the link, microphone and camera. Charge your device and think about what you'll do if your connection drops. If data or a private space is a problem, raise it early and ask what options are available.

Have your CV, the advert and a few brief notes nearby. If you plan to take notes during the conversation, say so. Keep confidential material from previous employers out of any work samples you share.

## If the interview is tomorrow

You can still prepare usefully. Read the advert, choose a few relevant examples and say your opening answer out loud. Check the employer's website, prepare two questions and confirm the logistics. Then give yourself time to rest.

Afterwards, make a quick note of the questions you found difficult and the next steps you were given. If the stated response date passes, a polite follow-up is reasonable. An unanswered message doesn't tell you exactly what happened inside the organisation.

For now, pick one experience and practise explaining it clearly. That's a much better starting point than trying to memorise forty perfect answers.
