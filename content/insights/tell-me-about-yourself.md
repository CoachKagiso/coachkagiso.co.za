# "Tell me about yourself": how to build a clear interview answer

You know yourself. You've been yourself your whole life. So why does this question suddenly make you forget everything you've ever done?

Part of the difficulty is how open it sounds. Are they asking about your qualifications? Your current job? Where you grew up? Should you start at the beginning?

For a job interview, give the person a short introduction to the experience that's relevant to the role. Think of it as helping them know where to begin the conversation with you.

You don't need to squeeze every achievement into the opening answer. Choose the details that make the next question easier to ask.

## Start with the work that's relevant now

Begin with a plain description of what you do or what you've been preparing to do. If you're between jobs, you can describe your experience without pretending you're currently employed.

"I have four years of experience in office support, mostly handling schedules, records and supplier follow-ups" gives someone a clear starting point.

Then add a relevant example or area of responsibility. Finish by connecting that experience to the opportunity in front of you. This gives your answer a direction instead of turning it into a spoken version of every line on your CV.

Around a minute is a useful practice starting point, but it isn't a rule for every interview. Follow any time limit you're given, and watch for the interviewer wanting to move into questions.

## If you're applying for your first professional role

The following is an illustrative example. The person and experience are invented for teaching purposes; use only details that are true for you.

"I've recently completed a diploma in business management, and I'm looking for an opportunity in office administration. During my final-year group project, I kept track of deadlines, organised our shared documents and followed up with the team when information was missing. I enjoyed the coordination side of the work. This role interests me because I'd be able to use those skills while learning how your team manages its day-to-day operations."

The answer names the qualification and explains an example. It also makes the limits of the experience clear. A student project is described as a student project.

You can draw on volunteering, part-time work or responsibilities in a community group too. Choose an example that relates to the advertised work. You don't need to begin with an apology about having "no experience" if you have something useful to discuss.

## If you've been working for several years

Here's another fictional example:

"I'm an office administrator with five years of experience supporting a busy operations team. My work includes coordinating diaries, keeping supplier documents up to date and preparing information for weekly meetings. More recently, I've been helping new colleagues learn those processes, which I've enjoyed. I'm interested in this senior administrator role because it would let me take on more responsibility for how the office runs."

This person doesn't list all five years month by month. They select responsibilities that explain their fit and give a reason for the next step.

If your strongest example includes an outcome you can verify, add it. You don't need to force a number into the answer. "The guide I wrote is now used to train new starters" says something useful on its own, provided it's true.

## If you're changing direction

A career change can make the question feel harder because you want to explain the move without spending the whole answer defending it.

Try a connection between your existing work and the direction you've researched. This example is also fictional:

"My background is in customer support, where I've spent several years helping people solve problems with orders and services. Part of that work involved explaining our systems to new colleagues. I became interested in training coordination through that experience, so I've been exploring the role and have completed a small practice project organising a training session. I'm interested in this position because it combines coordination with helping people learn, and I can bring practical experience of the questions staff face when using a system."

The answer explains the connection. It doesn't claim that customer support and training coordination are identical, or that a practice project replaces years of specialist experience.

If you're still unsure how to explain your move, work through the [career-change guide](/insights/career-change-south-africa-before-you-leave) before polishing the wording. Sometimes the sentence is difficult because the decision itself needs more thought.

## Choose what to leave out

You can share personal information when it feels relevant and comfortable. You don't need to provide your family history, private circumstances or every reason you've ever left a job in this opening answer.

If you've had a difficult experience with a previous employer, prepare a separate, measured answer for questions about leaving. Opening with the whole dispute can use up the time you need to explain what you bring to this role.

Also check for descriptions that anyone could use: "hardworking", "passionate", "a people's person". You may be all of those things. A short example helps the interviewer understand what those words mean in your working life.

## Write notes you can speak from

Take a page and complete these prompts:

- The experience I want them to understand first is...
- One example that connects to this role is...
- I'm interested in this opportunity because...

Say the answer aloud, then stop looking at the page and try again in your own words. If the wording changes but the meaning stays clear, that's fine. You're practising a conversation.

Record yourself once. Listen for parts where you get lost in background detail or make a claim without explaining it. Cut one unnecessary sentence and try again. You don't need to remove every pause or sound like a presenter.

If you go blank in the interview, take a breath and start with the plainest true sentence: "My background is in..." You can ask for a moment to gather your thoughts. An imperfect opening doesn't prevent you from giving useful answers afterwards.

## Prepare for the question that comes next

Anything you mention may lead to a follow-up. If you say you improved a process, be ready to explain what changed and what you personally did. If you mention a project, know enough about it to talk beyond the polished opening.

The [interview preparation guide](/insights/interview-preparation-south-africa) will help you build those examples. If you'd like to practise with someone, [book a free discovery call](/book/discovery).

For now, start with the experience you want this employer to understand. You already know the story. The work is choosing where to begin.
