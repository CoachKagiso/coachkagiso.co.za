# What are your salary expectations? How to answer in a South African interview

The conversation is going well. You've explained your experience, asked a thoughtful question and started imagining yourself in the role.

Then someone asks what you'd like to earn.

Suddenly you're trying to calculate your value, your monthly expenses and the risk of saying the wrong thing, all while looking perfectly relaxed on a video call.

You can prepare for this part too. You need a researched range, a clear understanding of what the numbers mean and a few words you feel comfortable saying. You don't need to guess the one magical figure that makes an employer say yes.

## Make sure you're discussing the same kind of number

Before comparing figures, clarify whether you're talking about monthly or annual pay, and whether the figure is gross salary or cost to company.

Gross pay is pay before deductions. Net pay is what remains after deductions. Cost to company, usually shortened to CTC, describes the employer's total package cost and may include contributions or benefits that won't arrive as cash in your account. The exact breakdown matters.

You can simply ask: "Is that figure monthly CTC, and could you talk me through what's included?"

There's no embarrassment in checking. Comparing your current take-home pay with a proposed CTC figure can make an offer look better than it is. If a term on a payslip or offer letter is unfamiliar, ask HR what it means before you compare figures.

## Research the role you're moving into

Look at recent adverts with salary ranges for comparable work. Match the responsibilities, experience level and location as closely as you can. A familiar title doesn't always mean a similar job.

Published salary guides can help you establish a starting point. Large job boards and specialist recruiters publish salary guides from time to time, often for a particular field. Check when the information was collected and whether the figures are monthly, annual, gross or CTC.

Keep a few notes: the source, its date, the kind of role and the stated range. If your research produces very different figures, investigate why. You may be comparing different levels of responsibility or very different packages.

Your current salary is one piece of information. The scope of the new role also matters, particularly if you've been underpaid or are taking on significantly different work. Avoid building your whole expectation around a fixed percentage increase.

## Decide what would work for you before the call

Privately, work out what you need to understand before accepting an offer. That includes the package itself and costs that may change, such as transport or childcare.

Keep your preferred range separate from the lowest offer you could realistically accept. You don't have to volunteer your minimum at the start of the conversation. And don't give a range whose lower end you already know won't work for you.

The figure you discuss with the employer should have a reason behind it: the role, your relevant experience and the evidence you've gathered. Your household costs matter to your decision, but the employer will also be assessing the position's budget and responsibilities.

## If they ask before you've heard enough about the role

Try: "I'd like to understand the responsibilities and package a little better before giving you a firm expectation. Could you share the range budgeted for the role?"

They may share it. They may ask you to give your expectation first. If you've done the research, you can give a range and explain what it's based on, while making clear that the full package still needs discussion.

You don't need to turn the conversation into a battle over who says a number first. Your aim is to find out whether the opportunity could work for both sides.

## If you're ready to give a range

Here's a fictional example of the wording, not a salary recommendation: "Based on the responsibilities we've discussed and the comparable roles I've researched, I'm looking at R30,000 to R34,000 monthly CTC. I'd like to understand the benefits and how the package is structured before making a final decision."

Use figures supported by your own research. Be prepared to explain the experience you bring, especially if you're asking for the upper end of the employer's range.

If they ask about your current package and you choose to discuss it, describe it accurately. Don't inflate a figure or invent benefits to strengthen your position. You can still explain why your expectation for the new role differs.

## Compare the whole offer

Imagine two fictional offers, each described as R30,000 monthly CTC. One includes R3,000 in employer retirement contributions within that total. The other has a different benefits structure. Those headline figures don't tell you what your gross cash pay or take-home pay would be.

Ask for a written breakdown. Check what is guaranteed, what depends on performance and which contributions sit inside the quoted total. A possible bonus shouldn't quietly become part of your guaranteed monthly budget.

Write down these questions for each offer:

- What is the guaranteed gross cash pay?
- Which benefits and employer contributions are included in the CTC figure?
- What deductions would apply to me?
- Is any commission or bonus guaranteed, and what are its conditions?
- What extra costs would I take on to do the job?

Ask payroll or HR to clarify the parts you don't understand. An estimated payslip can be helpful, although your actual deductions depend on your circumstances. Don't assume that dividing annual CTC by twelve gives your monthly take-home pay.

## If the offer is below what you expected

You can be appreciative and still ask a question: "Thank you for the offer. I'm interested in the role. Based on the scope and the experience I'd bring, I was hoping for a package closer to ___. Is there flexibility?"

Give them time to respond. If the salary can't change, ask about other terms that matter to you. Those might include working arrangements, development support or the timing and criteria for a review. A promised future review is not a guaranteed increase, so understand what is actually being offered.

You may decide the offer works for you. You may decide it doesn't. Either way, try to make the decision with the details in writing rather than the excitement or pressure of the call.
