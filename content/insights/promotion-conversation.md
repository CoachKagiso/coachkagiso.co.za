# How to ask for a promotion when you're already doing more than your job

You're the person people call when something goes wrong. You help the new starters. You cover for your manager. Somehow, extra responsibility keeps finding you.

But when you think about asking for a promotion, you hesitate. What if they say you're getting ahead of yourself? What if they remind you that everyone is working hard?

It can feel uncomfortable to put your own name forward, especially when you've built a reputation for being reliable and easy to work with. Still, your manager may not know what you want next. They may see the help you're giving without recognising how much your role has changed.

You can make that change easier to see. Start by getting clear about the role you want, then prepare a conversation about the work you've been doing and what would need to happen next.

## Name the move you're asking for

"I'd like to grow" leaves a lot open to interpretation. You might mean a better salary. Your manager might hear that you'd enjoy a training course.

Are you asking for a team leader position? A more senior role in the work you already do? A formal change to responsibilities you've been carrying for months?

A promotion and a salary review can be connected, but they aren't always the same conversation. If you want both, say so clearly. If there isn't a suitable next role in your organisation, you may first need a discussion about what progression could realistically look like.

Try writing one sentence before you book the meeting: "I'd like to discuss moving into ___ because my current work now includes ___."

If you can't finish it yet, find out more about the next role. Ask for the job description or speak to someone who understands its responsibilities. Being excellent in your current job matters. The next job may ask something different of you.

## Build a case you can fit on one page

You don't need a presentation with twenty slides. You need a few examples that make the change in your work clear.

For each example, describe what needed attention, what you personally did and what happened afterwards. Include the people who can confirm it or the work records that support it.

Here's an illustrative example. "I help the team" becomes: "When two new colleagues joined, I prepared a handover guide, walked them through the weekly reporting process and became their first contact for questions. The supervisor then used that guide for the next starter."

The second version explains the contribution. It doesn't pretend you managed a whole department. It also doesn't invent a number to sound impressive.

Choose examples that relate to the role you want. If you're asking to lead a team, include evidence of how you help others deliver, deal with problems and take responsibility for decisions. Working the longest hours isn't enough to explain readiness for management.

Your page can have four short sections: the role you're asking about, the relevant work you've done, the responsibilities you're ready to take on and any development you still need. You can acknowledge a gap without talking yourself out of the conversation.

## Ask for a proper meeting

A quick comment while your manager is leaving for another appointment probably won't get the attention this deserves.

You could write: "I'd like to set aside time to discuss my progression and the responsibilities I've taken on. I'm interested in the team leader role and would like your feedback on my readiness and the process for being considered. Would we be able to book half an hour?"

Use the actual role and the language that feels natural to you. You're asking for a work conversation, not a favour.

If your organisation has a review or promotion cycle, find out when decisions are prepared, who is involved and what information they need. The date of your performance review may be later than the date the budget was discussed.

## Say what you want, then leave room for an answer

In the meeting, make your request early. You don't need to spend fifteen minutes proving that you're grateful before you're allowed to talk about progression.

"I'd like to be considered for the team leader role. Over the past few months I've taken responsibility for these parts of the team's work. I'd like to understand how you assess my readiness and what the next steps would be."

Talk through your strongest examples. Then listen. Your manager may raise something you hadn't considered, such as a responsibility you haven't yet had a chance to demonstrate.

Ask for specifics. "What would good performance in that area look like?" is more useful than leaving with "I need to be more strategic" and no idea what that means on Monday morning.

## If the answer is "not yet"

Find out what "yet" depends on. Is there a skill to develop? Does a role need to become available? Is there a formal assessment or application process?

You can say: "I'd like to work on the gap you've identified. Can we agree on an opportunity to demonstrate it and a date to review the progress?"

Make sure the opportunity is realistic alongside your workload. Taking on unlimited extra work with no clear criteria can leave you exhausted and no closer to a decision.

If the answer is "no budget", ask whether your readiness and the funding decision are separate issues. You might agree on development work, a future review or a different responsibility. None of those is a confirmed promotion. Keep the distinction clear for yourself.

## Write down what was agreed

Send a short follow-up: "Thank you for the conversation. My understanding is that I'll work on ___, with support from ___. We'll review this on ___. The decision about ___ is still outstanding. Please let me know if I've missed anything."

If a promotion is offered, clarify the title, responsibilities, pay and effective date through the employer's formal process. Enthusiasm in a meeting is encouraging; you still need to understand the actual offer.

And if the conversation stays vague after repeated attempts? That's information you can use when planning your next move. You don't have to make a rushed decision, but you can start comparing your options with clearer eyes.
