# Before you start

## What this is

This is a workbook, not a book. You will write in it. If you only read it, it will not work.

By the end you will have built a bank of eight to ten interview stories drawn from your own working life, structured so that a panel can score them, and a one-page matrix that tells you which story to use for which question. That bank is reusable. You build it once and adapt it for every interview you sit in from here on.

## What this is not

**This is not live coaching.** Nobody is reading your CV. Nobody is giving you feedback. Everything in here you do yourself, which is exactly why it costs what it costs.

**This is not legal advice.** Part 7 covers what South African law says about questions you may be asked. It is written to help you recognise a problem and respond to it in the room. If you believe you have been discriminated against, speak to a labour lawyer or the CCMA, not to a workbook.

**This is not a script to memorise.** Memorised answers fail the moment a panel asks a follow-up question. What you are building here is material, not a performance.

**This is not a list of model answers.** There are no model answers in here, because the answers have to be yours. Somebody else's story will not survive one probing question.

## What you will have when you finish

- Eight to ten completed story cards in your own words
- A quantification for each story that is honest and defensible
- A story-by-competency matrix for one real job you are targeting
- A ninety-second opening answer built from two of your stories
- Five employer needs statements, each tied to a specific story
- A recording of yourself answering out loud, and a score for it

**Essential:**

# Part 1: How interviews are actually scored

## The thing nobody tells you

Most interview advice teaches you how to talk. Almost none of it teaches you how you are being marked. That is backwards, because the marking is the part that decides whether you get the job.

In a structured interview, the panel is not sitting there forming a general impression of whether they like you. They are working from a score sheet. Each question maps to a competency. Each competency has a rating scale. After you leave the room, each panellist assigns you a number, and those numbers get compared against the numbers given to everyone else who sat in that chair.

This matters for one practical reason. **If a panellist cannot write down a specific thing you did, they cannot score you.** Not a low score. No score. Your answer produces nothing for them to mark.

## What a rating scale looks like

Panels commonly use rating scales that are anchored to behaviour, meaning each number on the scale is tied to a description of what that level of evidence looks like. The exact wording differs between organisations, but the shape is consistent:

| Score | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| **1** | No relevant example given. The candidate described how they generally approach things, or spoke in principle. |
| **2** | An example was mentioned but was vague. No clear personal action. No outcome. |
| **3** | A real example with clear personal action, but the outcome is thin or missing. |
| **4** | A specific example, clear personal action, a real outcome, and evidence of judgement. |
| **5** | All of the above, plus complexity handled, and clear relevance to the job being filled. |

Read that table again and notice something. The difference between a 2 and a 4 is not confidence, charisma or how well you got on with the interviewer. It is specificity, personal action, and outcome.

That is good news. Those three things can be prepared.

## Why good stories still score badly

Four failure modes account for most lost marks. You will meet all four again in Part 5, where you will learn to audit for them.

**You answered in general terms.** The question was "tell me about a time", and you answered "what I usually do is". A general answer cannot be scored, no matter how sensible it is.

**You said "we" and never "I".** The panel is scoring you, not your former team. If they cannot work out which part was yours, they cannot credit you with it. There is more on this in Part 5, and it is not about bragging. It is about the score sheet.

**You never said what happened.** You described what you did and then stopped. Without an outcome, the panel has action but no evidence that the action worked.

**You told a good story about the wrong thing.** The question was about conflict and you told a strong story about hitting a deadline. It was a good story. It scored nothing, because it produced no evidence for the competency being marked.

# Part 2: The method

## STAR, and the part everyone leaves out

You have probably met STAR before. Situation, Task, Action, Result. It is a way of structuring an answer so that it contains the things a panel needs to score.

It is useful. It is also over-sold. You will see claims that research proves STAR answers get you hired. That is not quite what the research says. What is well established is that structured interviews, meaning interviews where the employer asks consistent questions and scores against defined criteria, predict job performance better than unstructured chats. That is about how employers run interviews, not about which acronym candidates use in their heads.

So use STAR as scaffolding, not as a magic word. And add the step that most people leave out.

## The four parts, honestly weighted

**Situation.** Where and when. Keep it short. This is the part candidates over-invest in, because it is the easiest part to talk about. Two sentences.

**Task.** What you were responsible for, and why it mattered. One sentence. Be careful here: describe what you decided to take on, not just what you were told to do. "I was asked to" scores lower than "I realised we needed to".

**Action.** What you personally did, step by step. **This is where the marks are.** Half your answer should live here. If your situation is longer than your action, your answer is upside down.

**Result.** What changed. A number if you honestly have one. An honest description if you do not. Part 4 covers how to find a defensible number when you never tracked anything.

## The Link

Here is the step that turns a good story into a scoring answer.

After the result, in one or two sentences, you connect the story to what this employer needs.

Not "and that is why I am a hard worker". That is a claim about you. The Link is a claim about them.

**Three patterns that work:**

**The problem pattern.** "I am telling you this because your advert mentions [the challenge they named]. That is the same problem I was solving there, and it is the part of this role I would want to start with."

**The constraint pattern.** "What that experience taught me is how to deliver when the resources are not there. From what you have described about this team, that seems relevant."

**The values pattern.** "That is also why the way you have described how this department works appealed to me, because it is the same approach I took there."

The Link works because it does the panel's job for them. They are trying to decide whether your past behaviour predicts your future performance in their role. Most candidates leave them to make that leap alone. You make it out loud.

**A caution.** Use the Link once per answer, at the end. Used more often, it sounds like a sales pitch and the authenticity drops away.

## Worked example

*Here is the same story told twice. Read both.*

### Weak version

> "So in my previous role we had a lot of pressure on the month-end reporting. It was always a stressful time and there was a lot of back and forth with the different departments who never submitted on time. We came up with a way to improve it and it worked out much better after that. It was a good team effort."

Score that against the table in Part 1. There is a situation. There is no clear personal action, no outcome, and no link. On most panel score sheets that is a 2.

### Strong version

> "At my previous company, month-end reporting consistently ran three to four days late. The delay was not in finance, it was that four departments submitted their numbers in different formats and somebody had to reconcile them by hand.
>
> I was not asked to fix this, but it was costing me two days a month, so I took it on.
>
> I sat with each of the four department heads and worked out what they were actually able to produce without extra work. Then I built one template that accepted all four inputs. I ran it in parallel with the old process for two cycles so nobody had to trust it blindly, and I trained one person in each department on it.
>
> After that, month-end closed on the second working day instead of the fifth or sixth. That was consistent for the eleven months I remained in the role. I did not have a formal KPI on it, but the two days a month it saved me were the two days I had been losing.
>
> I am telling you this one because your advert mentions consolidating reporting across the regions. That is the same problem at a bigger scale, and it is the part of this role I would want to start with."

Same story. Same person. The second version has a specific incident, dense personal action, a stated result with an honest note about the absence of a formal metric, and a link to the employer's actual need.

**Notice what the strong version does not do.** It does not invent a percentage. It does not claim the whole thing was the speaker's idea. It does not say "we". It says what was done, what happened, and why it is relevant here.

## How long should an answer be?

You will see confident numbers quoted for this. Ninety seconds. Two minutes. There is no solid research behind any specific figure, so treat them as rules of thumb rather than science.

The practical guidance is this. **Aim to finish somewhere between ninety seconds and two minutes, then stop and let them probe.** Panels work to a schedule. A four-minute answer eats the time in which you could have given two more pieces of evidence. Stopping early is almost never punished. Running long frequently is.

The useful test is not the clock. It is this: **have you given them a specific incident, your actions, and an outcome?** If yes, stop talking.

# Part 3: The nine story types

## Why these nine

You will find lists online offering anywhere from five to fifteen "essential interview stories". None of them are based on measurement. Nobody has counted, across a large sample of real interviews, how often each category actually comes up. If a workbook tells you these nine types cover eighty percent of questions, it is guessing.

So here is the honest basis for the nine below. They are not organised around types of anecdote. They are organised around **the competencies employers write on their score sheets**. That is a better organising principle, because it means when you read a job advert you can work out which of your stories to bring, rather than hoping the right anecdote comes to mind.

Nine is a working number. Eight is enough. Ten is comfortable. Build six before you worry about the rest.

## How to use this part

Do not read all nine and then start writing. You will lose momentum and finish nothing.

**Read type one. Then go to Part 4 and find a story for it. Then come back.** The types are here as a reference you will return to, not as a chapter to get through.

---

### Type 1: Delivering under pressure

**What is being tested:** whether you produce results when conditions are poor.

**Question stems you will hear:**
- Tell me about a time you had to deliver with less than you needed.
- Describe a situation where you were under significant time pressure.
- Give me an example of a deadline you were at risk of missing.

**What a strong answer shows:** that you prioritised deliberately, that you made a call about what to drop, and that something concrete was delivered.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- When did you last work a weekend or a very long day, and what caused it?
- When did you have to tell somebody they could not have all of what they asked for?
- What is the tightest turnaround you have ever met?

---

### Type 2: Problem solving

**What is being tested:** how you think, not just what you produced.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a complex problem you solved.
- Describe a time you had to make a decision without full information.
- Walk me through how you approached something you had not done before.

**What a strong answer shows:** a visible process. The panel wants to hear how you narrowed it down, not just that you got there.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- When did something break and nobody knew why?
- When did you have to work out the cause of a recurring problem?
- What is something you fixed that had been going wrong for a while?

---

### Type 3: Conflict or a difficult person

**What is being tested:** whether you stay professional when a person, not a task, is the problem.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager.
- Describe a difficult colleague and how you handled them.
- Give an example of a client or stakeholder who was hard to work with.

**What a strong answer shows:** that you separated the person from the problem, that you took a step rather than complaining, and that you can describe the other person fairly.

**Warning:** this is the story most likely to sink you. If the other person comes across as entirely unreasonable and you come across as entirely blameless, the panel does not believe you. Include the part where you understood their position.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- Who was the hardest person you have worked with, and what was one specific occasion with them?
- When did you last have to push back on somebody senior to you?
- When did a working relationship improve because of something you did?

---

### Type 4: Working with others without authority

**What is being tested:** whether you can get things done through people who do not report to you. Most work is this.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a time you needed cooperation from another department.
- Describe a project where you had responsibility but no authority.
- Give an example of how you influenced a decision.

**What a strong answer shows:** that you understood what the other person needed, and traded rather than demanded.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- When did you need something from somebody who had no reason to help you?
- When did you change somebody's mind?
- When did you get a group to agree on something?

---

### Type 5: Leadership

**What is being tested:** ownership and direction. You do not need a management title for this.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a time you took the lead on something.
- Describe how you motivated a team through a difficult period.
- Give an example of something you took on that was not your job.

**What a strong answer shows:** that you stepped forward before being asked, and that other people followed.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- When did you take charge of something because nobody else was going to?
- Who have you trained, mentored or brought up to speed?
- When did people come to you rather than to the person officially in charge?

---

### Type 6: Failure, mistake or hard feedback

**What is being tested:** self-awareness and accountability. This question is not a trap, but a bad answer is fatal.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a time you made a significant mistake.
- Describe a project that did not go well.
- Tell me about difficult feedback you received.

**What a strong answer shows:** a real failure with real consequences, honest ownership, and a specific change afterwards.

**Three ways people get this wrong:**
- Choosing a fake failure. "I care too much about quality" scores a 1.
- Choosing a catastrophic failure that raises new concerns.
- Owning the mistake but never saying what changed afterwards.

**The right size of failure:** real enough that it cost something, contained enough that it did not end anyone's career, and old enough that you have visibly done things differently since.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- What is the last thing you got wrong at work?
- What feedback have you received more than once?
- What would you do differently if you could repeat one project?

---

### Type 7: Change, ambiguity or a new environment

**What is being tested:** adaptability. Increasingly common as a question.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a time priorities changed suddenly.
- Describe joining a new team or organisation.
- Give an example of learning something new quickly.

**What a strong answer shows:** that you adjusted without waiting to be told how, and kept delivering during the disruption.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- What is the biggest change you have lived through at work? Restructure, new system, new leadership, retrenchments?
- What did you have to learn fast, and how did you go about it?
- When did your job change underneath you?

---

### Type 8: Communication with a difficult audience

**What is being tested:** whether you can carry information to somebody who does not want it or does not understand it.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news.
- Describe explaining something technical to a non-technical audience.
- Give an example of a presentation or difficult conversation.

**What a strong answer shows:** that you adjusted how you communicated based on who was listening.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- When did you have to tell somebody something they did not want to hear?
- When did you have to explain your work to somebody outside your field?
- When was something you said misunderstood, and what did you do about it?

---

### Type 9: Integrity, or customer and service

*Choose one of these two depending on the roles you are targeting. Integrity for public sector, finance, audit, compliance and senior roles. Customer for service, sales, retail, hospitality and front-line roles. Build both if you have time.*

**Integrity: what is being tested:** whether you hold a line under pressure.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a time you were asked to do something you were not comfortable with.
- Describe a time you had to raise a concern.
- Give an example of choosing the harder correct option.

**Customer: what is being tested:** whether you take responsibility for somebody else's experience.

**Question stems:**
- Tell me about a difficult customer or client.
- Describe a time you went beyond what was required.
- Give an example of recovering a relationship that had gone wrong.

**Prompts to find yours:**
- When did you raise something that was easier to ignore?
- When did you fix something that was not your fault?
- When did somebody thank you specifically?

# Part 4: Finding your stories

## Why you cannot remember anything

This is the hardest part of the workbook, and it catches almost everybody. You sit down to list your achievements and your mind produces nothing.

There are three reasons for this, and none of them is that you have not achieved anything.

**You dismissed it as normal.** The thing you fixed became the way things are done, so it stopped feeling like an achievement. It was still an achievement. The panel has never seen it before.

**You are searching the wrong way.** "List your accomplishments" is an almost impossible instruction. Memory does not work by category. It works by occasion, place, person and object.

**People are generally poor judges of their own performance.** This is well established. It is why prompts that point at external evidence, such as what somebody said to you afterwards, work far better than prompts that ask you to rate yourself.

The fix is to stop searching your memory and start searching the evidence trail.

## The evidence sweep

*Set a timer for twenty minutes. Do not write stories during this. Only collect.*

Go and look at whichever of these you can still reach:

- **Old performance reviews and development plans.** The single highest-yield source. Somebody already wrote down what you did well.
- **Your own CV.** Every bullet on it was a real event. What actually happened behind that line?
- **Old emails.** Search your sent items for "thank you", "well done", "sorted", "fixed", "apologies". Search your inbox for your own name.
- **Project documents.** Kick-off packs, close-out reports, handover notes, incident reports.
- **Your calendar from twelve to twenty-four months ago.** Meetings you had forgotten. Deadlines. Launches.
- **WhatsApp work groups.** A genuine memory trail. Be mindful of other people's privacy when you use anything from it.
- **Bonus or increase motivations you wrote.** You already argued your own case once.
- **Certificates, training records, learnership documents.**

As you go, write down incident titles. Nothing else.

## Critical incident prompts

If the sweep did not fill twelve, use these. They come from a research method that has been used for decades to get people to recall specific work events, and the principle behind it is simple: **ask for one occasion, never for a general habit.**

Notice that every prompt below asks for the last time, or one specific time. That is deliberate. Do not soften them into "how do you usually".

- The last time somebody senior thanked you specifically.
- The last time something went badly wrong and you were the one who dealt with it.
- The last time you were asked to do something you did not know how to do.
- The last time you disagreed with a decision and said so.
- The last time somebody asked for your help because of something you knew.
- The first week of your most recent job. What was broken that you noticed?
- The last thing you built, wrote, designed or set up that other people still use.
- The last time a deadline was genuinely at risk.
- The last time you had to apologise at work.
- The last time you trained somebody.
- The last time you saved money or time for somebody else.
- The last time you were the only person in the room who raised something.

## If your experience is not corporate

A lot of good evidence sits outside formal employment, and imported workbooks tend to ignore it entirely. That is a mistake, and in the South African labour market it is a serious one.

**Translate what you have already done.**

| If you did this | You have evidence for |
|---|---|
| Ran a side business, however small | Costing, pricing, customer management, supplier negotiation, cash flow |
| Sold at a market or online | Sales, stock management, customer service, pricing |
| Organised a community event, funeral, wedding or fundraiser | Project management, budgeting, stakeholder coordination, logistics |
| Ran a stokvel or society | Financial administration, trust, record keeping, conflict handling |
| Volunteered at a school, church or NGO | Whatever you actually did there, described in work language |
| Held a leadership position at university or college | Leadership, representation, negotiation, event delivery |
| Cared for a family member during a gap | Coordination, scheduling, financial management, resilience |
| Did piece work, contract work or informal work | Delivery under constraint, self-management, client handling |

**How to use this without over-claiming.** Do not dress it up as something it was not. Say plainly what it was and then say what it required. "I ran a small delivery business for eighteen months. I was doing my own costing, and I learnt very quickly that I was pricing below what the fuel was costing me. Fixing that is why the business survived its second year." That is a real answer about commercial awareness and it will hold up under questioning, because it is true.

## Finding a number when you never tracked one

Panels reward specificity. But you must never invent a figure. Apart from the honesty problem, an invented number collapses the moment somebody asks how you measured it.

Work down this ladder. Stop at the first rung you can honestly stand on.

**Rung 1: The real number.** You have it, or you can still get it. Use it.

**Rung 2: A before and after you can describe.** "It used to take five days. Afterwards it took two." You are not claiming a percentage. You are describing an observable change.

**Rung 3: Scale and scope.** How many people, how many sites, how many transactions, what size team, what period. "A team of six, across three provinces, for eighteen months." This is factual and it gives the panel a sense of weight.

**Rung 4: Third-party evidence.** Something outside your own opinion. The audit finding that closed. The client that renewed. The complaint that stopped. The system that has run since. The person who was promoted.

**Rung 5: The honest unmeasured outcome.** "We never had a formal measure on it. What I can tell you is that the process I set up is still the one they use." This scores better than you expect, because it demonstrates that you know the difference between evidence and assertion.

### Say this, never that

| Never say | Say instead |
|---|---|
| "I improved efficiency by 40%." (invented) | "It went from taking most of a day to taking about two hours." |
| "I saved the company millions." (unverifiable) | "The contract I renegotiated came in below the previous one. I do not have the exact figure in front of me, but it was a meaningful difference on a large account." |
| "I increased sales significantly." (vague) | "I took over a territory that had not hit target in four quarters, and we hit it in my second quarter." |

**The rule:** never state a number you could not explain the source of if somebody asked you the follow-up question. Assume they will ask.

# Part 5: Building the bank

This is the production part of the workbook. Everything so far has been preparation. Now you write the cards.

## The order to build in

Do not start with the hardest one. Build in this order:

1. **Delivering under pressure.** Everybody has one, and it builds momentum.
2. **Problem solving.** Usually the second easiest.
3. **Working without authority.**
4. **Leadership.**
5. **Change or ambiguity.**
6. **Difficult communication.**
7. **Conflict.**
8. **Integrity or customer.**
9. **Failure.** Last, deliberately. By this point you will have a much better sense of what a complete story looks like, and this is the one that most needs that.

## The "I" line

Before you write the cards, one thing that will change how they score.

The panel is marking you. Not your team. When your answer is entirely in the first person plural, the panel physically cannot attribute the competency to you, so they mark you down. This is not a judgement about your character. It is an administrative consequence of how the score sheet works.

Many people are uncomfortable with this, and for good reason. Claiming individual credit for team work can feel dishonest, and in a lot of workplaces it is genuinely bad behaviour. Nobody is asking you to become that person.

**Here is the structure that solves it.** Say all three parts, in this order:

> **"The team delivered [outcome]. The part I owned was [your specific contribution]. That is what produced [specific result]."**

You have credited the team. You have given the panel something to score. Both are true statements. You have not taken anything from anybody.

Write this line for every card. It is the single highest-value sentence on the page.

# Part 7: The South African context

Most interview advice you will read online is American. Some of it transfers. A meaningful amount of it is wrong here, and the legal section is wrong in ways that could matter to you. This part covers what actually differs.

## Terminology

Using the local term signals that you know the environment. Using the American term does not disqualify you, but it is a small unnecessary cost.

| Say this | Not this |
|---|---|
| CV | Resume |
| Retrenchment | Layoff |
| Package, or cost to company | Salary (when discussing the total) |
| Matric, or National Senior Certificate | High school diploma |
| NQF level | Degree "years" |
| Notice period | Two weeks' notice |
| Provident fund, pension, medical aid | Benefits, 401k |
| Learnership | Apprenticeship or internship (a learnership is a specific NQF-registered programme) |
| Labour broker, or TES | Staffing agency |
| EXCO, or board | Leadership team, C-suite |

## Who is in the room

South African hiring, particularly in the public sector, at state-owned entities, at universities and in large corporates, is heavily panel-based. You should expect three or more people.

**The chair or line manager.** Usually the person the role reports to. Asks most of the competency questions. This is the person you will instinctively play to.

**The HR representative.** Asks about process, package, notice period, gaps and reasons for leaving. Often flatter in tone. This is procedure, not hostility.

**The specialist or cross-functional panellist.** Somebody from finance, technical, operations or a partner department. Frequently says very little, then asks one sharp question.

**The scribe.** Somebody may be taking detailed notes without asking anything.

**In the public sector,** there may also be a union or employment equity observer whose role is to check that the process is fair.

### Three things this changes about how you answer

**Look at everybody.** Address the person who asked, but let your eyes move across the panel while you answer. The quiet ones are scoring you too.

**Do not write off the silent panellist.** The person who has said nothing for twenty minutes very often asks the hardest question in the session. Stay switched on for the whole interview.

**Expect to be interrupted less and probed more.** Panels work through a fixed question list. That is why the answer that stops at ninety seconds and invites a follow-up performs better than the one that runs for four minutes.

## Interviewing in your second or third language

Most professional interviews in South Africa are conducted in English, which for the majority of the country is not a first language. This is a real and under-discussed disadvantage, because it is easy to be marked down for a "vague" answer when the actual problem was reaching for a word.

Some honesty about the evidence here. There is research internationally on accent and non-native speaker bias in evaluative settings. Local research specific to South African interview panels is thin. So treat what follows as practical technique rather than proven science.

**Signpost.** Say the structure out loud. "The situation was this. What I did was this. The result was this." Signposting does two things: it helps the panel follow you, and it gives you a frame to hold on to when the vocabulary is not arriving.

**Prepare the vocabulary in advance, not live.** Go through your story cards and identify the five or six technical or specialised words you will need. Know them cold. Translating while under pressure is what costs you fluency.

**Use simple, strong verbs.** "I rebuilt the process" carries more evidence than "I facilitated an optimisation initiative". Plain language is not lesser language. In an evidence-based interview it is better, because evidence transfers more cleanly.

**Slow down slightly.** Not dramatically. Interview nerves speed everybody up, and speed is what makes second-language delivery harder to follow.

**Ask for the question again.** "Could you repeat that?" or "Just to make sure I have understood, are you asking about..." Every candidate is entitled to this and it reads as care, not weakness.

**Do not apologise for your English.** It draws attention to something the panel may not have registered as an issue.

## Difficult positioning

### Retrenchment

Retrenchment in South Africa is a dismissal based on the employer's operational requirements. It is a no-fault process, it is common, and it is widely understood by South African employers as an economic event rather than a reflection on the person.

That said, prepare a clean version. Do not assume it will be waved through.

**The shape: brief, factual, no bitterness, forward-facing.**

> "The company lost its largest contract at the end of the financial year and restructured. My whole department was affected, myself included. It was not a performance matter, and I have got the letter if that is ever useful. What I took from it is [something real]. Since then I have been [current activity], and what I am looking for now is [forward statement]."

**What sinks this answer:** explaining at length, criticising the former employer, or sounding as though you are still processing it. If it is still raw, that is completely reasonable, and it is exactly why you rehearse this one out loud before you need it.

### Gaps

Account for the time, briefly, and move forward.

> "I took [period] out to [reason]. During that time I [anything current: studying, freelance, caring, volunteering, a short course]. I have been actively looking since [date], and I am ready to start immediately."

You are not obliged to disclose medical detail. "I had a health matter which is fully resolved" is a complete answer. Under POPIA there are limits on what personal information an employer may collect and process, and your medical history is treated as a special category.

### Short tenures and contract work

Contract, fixed-term and project work are normal in South Africa. The problem is not the pattern, it is when a CV does not explain it.

**Label contract roles as contracts on your CV.** Half this problem disappears in the document. Then in the room: "Those were fixed-term contracts, which is standard in that sector. Each one ran to completion. What I am looking for now is somewhere permanent."

### Career change

Lead with the transferable competency, and give proof.

> "I moved from [field] to [field] because [genuine reason, framed forward not away]. The part that carries over directly is [competency], and here is where I have already used it in this context: [proof]."

Never criticise the field you left. It reads as somebody who might criticise this one next.

### Overqualified

They are usually worried about two things: that you will leave when something better arrives, and that they cannot afford you. Answer both, unprompted.

> "I understand why that comes up. On package, I know what this role pays and I am comfortable with it. On whether I would stay, what I want at this stage is [genuine reason]. I am not treating this as a stopgap."

### Returning after a break

Show currency. Anything recent counts: a short course, freelance work, volunteering, a certification. Reading, on its own, is weak evidence unless it produced something.

## Package and expectations

Being asked about your current earnings and your expectations is normal practice in South Africa. There is no general legislation preventing an employer from asking.

**Prepare three numbers before the interview:**

1. Your current or most recent total package, including provident fund, medical aid contribution and any guaranteed thirteenth cheque. Know the total cost to company figure, not just what lands in your account.
2. The market range for this role. Recruitment agency salary surveys, industry reports and job adverts for similar roles are all reasonable sources.
3. Your genuine minimum. The number below which you would decline.

**If asked early, you can defer once:**

> "I am comfortable discussing package. Before I anchor on a number, could I ask what range has been budgeted for the role? That way I can tell you honestly whether we are in the same area."

If they press, give a range rather than a single figure, and make it a range you would genuinely accept at the bottom end.

**Never inflate your current package.** It is verifiable through payslips and offer letters, and being caught ends the process immediately.

## Questions you may not have to answer

**This section is not legal advice.** It is written to help you recognise a problem and handle it in the room.

South African law protects you against unfair discrimination in hiring. The main instruments are section 9 of the Constitution, and the Employment Equity Act. Section 6 of the Employment Equity Act prohibits unfair discrimination, directly or indirectly, on a list of grounds that includes race, gender, sex, pregnancy, marital status, family responsibility, ethnic or social origin, colour, sexual orientation, age, disability, religion, HIV status, conscience, belief, political opinion, culture, language and birth.

The Act also restricts medical testing, and specifically prohibits testing an employee for HIV to determine employment eligibility unless a court has authorised it. It restricts psychological and similar assessments unless the instrument has been scientifically shown to be valid, reliable, applied fairly, and not biased against any group.

Separately, the Protection of Personal Information Act limits what personal information an employer may collect and process, and treats information about health, race, religion and similar categories as requiring specific justification.

**A note on statutes you may see quoted wrongly.** Section 187 of the Labour Relations Act is frequently cited in online articles about interview questions. It deals with automatically unfair dismissal, not recruitment. The relevant instruments for hiring are the Employment Equity Act and the Constitution.

### Three categories, kept separate

**Unlawful risk.** Questions used to screen you out on a protected ground. Whether you are planning to have children. Your HIV status. Your religion or political affiliation used as a filter. Disability enquiries that go beyond what is needed to assess whether you can do the job, or to arrange reasonable accommodation.

**Inadvisable, but not automatically unlawful.** Your age, framed as small talk. Your marital status. Whether your partner supports the hours. These are frequently asked without ill intent and are still poor practice.

**Uncomfortable, but perfectly lawful.** Why you left. The gap. Your current package. A hard probe on a weak answer. These are legitimate and you should prepare for them rather than object to them.

**Lawful where it is genuinely an inherent requirement of the job.** A specific language for a role that requires it. Physical requirements for physically demanding work. A security clearance. The test is whether the requirement is genuinely inherent to the job, not merely convenient.

### How to handle it in the room

You have three options, and the right one depends on how much you want the job and how the question was asked.

**Answer narrowly and move on.** Often the most practical choice. For "are you planning to have children": *"I am fully available for the role and the travel it involves. Should I take you through how I have managed that in the past?"* You have not answered the question. You have answered the concern underneath it.

**Redirect to the job.** *"I am not sure how that relates to the role, but if the concern is about availability, I am happy to speak to that."* Polite, firm, gives them a graceful exit.

**Decline.** *"I would rather not go into that. I am comfortable discussing anything relating to my ability to do the job."* You are entitled to this. Be aware it may change the temperature of the room, and decide in advance whether you mind.

**A judgement worth making.** If a panel asks something unlawful, they have told you something useful about the organisation. You are allowed to use that.

# Part 8: In the room

## What happens before the panel

Most South African corporate processes run in stages. Expect some version of:

1. **Application and screening.** Often automated filtering on qualification and experience.
2. **Recruiter or HR screening call.** Fifteen to thirty minutes. Availability, package, notice period, a broad sense of your background. Use two story hooks, not full stories.
3. **The panel interview.** This is what you built the bank for.
4. **A second round.** Frequently with a more senior person, sometimes technical, sometimes an assessment centre or psychometric testing.
5. **References and background checks.** Qualifications, credit and criminal checks are common.

**Reuse the bank at every stage.** The same nine stories carry you through all of it. What changes is the depth, not the material.

## The first minute

You are being read before you answer anything. Not to the extent that folklore suggests, and be sceptical of anybody quoting a precise statistic about how fast decisions are made, because those numbers rarely trace to a real study. But the opening does set a tone.

Practical, and enough: arrive early, greet each panellist individually, sit when invited, put your phone away entirely, and have your printed matrix and a pen on the table. Having notes is not cheating. It reads as preparation.

## Surviving the probe

Panels hire on the follow-up, not the first answer. Your cards have three probes prepared. Two more things to know:

**Silence after your answer is not a bad sign.** It usually means somebody is writing. Do not fill it. Candidates lose more marks in the panic after finishing than in the answer itself.

**"Can you give me another example?" is not a rejection.** It usually means the competency matters enough that they want a second data point. Your backup line handles it.

## If it goes wrong

**If you ramble,** stop and re-anchor: *"Let me be more concise. The key point is [outcome]."*

**If you go blank,** buy time honestly: *"Let me think about that properly for a second."* Then take the second. This reads far better than filler.

**If you give a bad answer,** you may come back to it: *"Could I come back to your earlier question? I do not think I gave you the best example."* Panels rarely refuse, and the recovery itself is evidence of self-awareness.

**If you realise mid-answer that it is the wrong story,** finish it briefly and add: *"Although a closer example to what you are asking about is..."*

## Questions to ask them

You will be invited to ask questions. This is scored. "No, I think you have covered everything" wastes it.

Prepare three. Choose from these and personalise them.

**On the role:**
- If we sat down in twelve months and you told me this had been a very good appointment, what would I have delivered?
- What is the biggest constraint the team is working under at the moment?
- Why is the role open?

**On how you would be measured:**
- How is performance measured in this team?
- What does the first three months look like?

**On the environment:**
- How does the team handle it when something goes wrong?
- What is the thing people find hardest about working here?

**For public sector roles:**
- How does the department measure delivery against its current service targets?

**The closing question,** if the conversation has gone well:
- Based on what we have discussed, is there anything about my experience you are uncertain about? I would rather clarify it now than leave you wondering.

**What not to ask in a first interview:** leave, working hours, whether you can work from home, or anything you would have known from the advert. Wait for the offer stage.

## Make your CV and your stories agree

A quick pass that prevents an avoidable problem.

- [ ] Every significant CV bullet maps to a story card, or should be reconsidered
- [ ] Dates, titles and reporting lines are accurate and consistent
- [ ] Anything described as a contract is labelled as a contract
- [ ] Your referees know they are listed, and know which three things you would like them to be able to speak to

That last point matters more than people expect. Offers are lost at reference stage when a referee describes a smaller role than the one the candidate described.

## Exercise 8: Record yourself

This is the last exercise and the one people skip. Do not skip it. Everything you have built so far exists on paper. Interviews happen out loud, and the gap between the two is larger than you think.

### What you need

**For playback,** anything that plays audio: a phone, a laptop, a desktop, a tablet.

**For recording,** anything that records: the voice recorder app on any phone, your laptop's built-in recorder, or a tablet.

**You need two devices only if you are running the panel simulation** in the premium pack, because one device plays the audio while the other records you. For this exercise, one device is enough. Record yourself answering, then play it back on the same device.

**If you only have one phone and you want to run the simulation:**

- Play the audio on a laptop, desktop or tablet, and record on your phone
- Or play on your phone through its speaker, and borrow a second phone to record
- Or use a friend's or family member's phone for recording
- Or play the audio on one device through a Bluetooth speaker and record on the phone

**Use the speaker, not headphones.** You want to hear the questions coming into the room, the way they will in an interview. Headphones make it feel like a podcast.

### Setting up

- **A quiet room where you can close the door.** You need to be able to speak at full volume without an audience. This is the actual requirement, and for many people it is the hardest one to arrange.
- **A table and a chair.** Sit up, as you would in the room. Do not do this lying on a bed. Posture changes your voice.
- **Your story cards and matrix printed or on a screen,** for the first run only.
- **A timer,** or just use your recording's own length.
- **Charge your devices first, and download the audio before you start.** If you are running the full simulation, load shedding halfway through a twenty-seven minute file is an avoidable irritation. Download once and play offline. It also saves your data.
